\section{Outpacing DRAM Refresh} \label{sec:fast}
    To achieve "\textit{\hyperref[ch:fast]{C3. Outpacing DRAM Refresh}}", memory accesses must be issued at a rate that 
    exceeds the \gls{dram} refresh behavior, ensuring sufficiently frequent activation's of the same \gls{dram} rows. 
    This section therefore analyzes access and refresh characteristics, examines the impact of existing 
    mitigation mechanisms and the extent to which they must be bypassed to support effective hammering 
    on ARM-based systems such as the Raspberry Pi devices.

    \subsection{Mitigations} \label{subsec:mitigations}
        % In General
        The Rowhammer vulnerability has been demonstrated in a wide range of environments, including sandboxed 
        systems, native platforms, virtual machines, JavaScript contexts, mobile devices, and even across 
        networks~\cite{paperHalfDouble}. Mitigation strategies aim to reduce both the likelihood and the impact of Rowhammer-induced 
        disturbance errors by altering system behavior, memory management policies, or \gls{dram} operation. Numerous 
        mitigation techniques have been proposed to address the Rowhammer problem in general~\cite{paperTriggeringRHHardwareFault}. 
        These approaches can be broadly classified into two categories: software-based protections and 
        hardware-based protections~\cite{paperTRRScope, paperDrammer}.
        
        \subsubsection{Software-based Defenses} \label{subsubsec:software-based-defenses}
            Software-based defenses against Rowhammer attacks are often complex, costly, and may remain incomplete~\cite{paperTRRespass}. 
            These approaches can be further divided into prevention or restriction techniques and detection strategies. 
            \\
            \textbf{Prevention and restriction approaches} aim to constrain or disable memory access patterns and instructions 
            that can lead to high activation rates, thereby preventing Rowhammer conditions from occurring. Examples 
            include \textit{MASCAT}~\cite{paperMASCAT}, which performs static analysis of binaries to detect suspicious instruction sequences, 
            though this approach faces open problems such as false positives. \textit{Instruction blacklisting} restricts access 
            to specific commands, such as cache flush instructions, to limit potential exploitation. Additionally, 
            \textit{B-CATT} disables vulnerable physical memory or \gls{dram} regions to prevent Rowhammer-induced errors~\cite{paperCantTouchThis, webAnotherFlip}.
            \\
            \textbf{Detection approaches} focus on identifying behaviors indicative of Rowhammer activity. This includes monitoring 
            abnormal activation frequencies or microarchitectural patterns so that countermeasures can be triggered before 
            bitflips occur. Rowhammer attacks are characterized by a significantly higher number of cache misses and hits 
            compared to other attack types. Detection systems also aim to identify hammering patterns, frequent row 
            activations, high cache-miss rates (as in \textit{ANVIL}~\cite{paperANVIL}), and other suspicious instruction sequences.
        
        \subsubsection{Hardware-based Defenses} \label{subsubsec:hardware-based-defenses}
            The root cause of Rowhammer stems from disturbance errors arising from physical interactions between multiple 
            cells in highly scaled \gls{dram}. Increased integration density and smaller cell geometries reduce noise margins 
            and exacerbate charge leakage effects. A straightforward hardware-based remedy would involve reducing \gls{dram} 
            cell density or increasing the physical cell size. Even so, this would require higher voltages and larger 
            charge reservoirs, resulting in unacceptable power and performance costs. Lowering integration density to 
            mitigate interference also significantly reduces memory capacity, creating a fundamental trade-off between 
            security and storage density. Since commercial \gls{dram} must maximize usable capacity, the underlying physical 
            disturbance mechanism cannot be entirely eliminated in current technology nodes. Moreover, hardware-based 
            approaches may introduce additional latency, increase energy consumption, or raise implementation complexity.
            \\
            \textbf{\gls{ecc}} can correct single-bit errors using stored parity information~\cite{paperRHARetrospective, webTenYearsOfRH, paperECC}. However, 
            \gls{ecc} can only detect and correct single-bitflips and is insufficient for addressing multi-bitflips, which 
            have become increasingly common~\cite{paperTriggeringRHHardwareFault}.
            \\
            \textbf{Increasing the \gls{dram} refresh rate} represents another mitigation strategy. \gls{dram} cells are volatile and 
            require refreshing at standard intervals of 32 ms to 64 ms~\cite{paperTriggeringRHHardwareFault}. The simplest approach is to increase 
            the refresh rate by two to four times~\cite{paperRHARetrospective, webTenYearsOfRH}, which reduces charge leakage exposure. Nonetheless, higher refresh 
            frequencies delay data access, negatively impacting system performance~\cite{webTenYearsOfRH}. While this strategy makes Rowhammer 
            attacks more difficult, it is often unacceptable due to its detrimental effects on performance and energy efficiency~\cite{paperTriggeringRHHardwareFault}.
            \\
            \textbf{\gls{trr}} detects frequently activated rows and refreshes adjacent rows to prevent 
            disturbance errors~\cite{paperTRRScope, paperTRRespass}. Nonetheless, \textit{\gls{trr}} is vendor-specific and non-mandatory, and it is vulnerable to bypasses such 
            as \textit{TRRespass}~\cite{paperTRRespass,toolTRRespass} and \textit{Halfdouble}~\cite{paperHalfDouble,toolHalfDouble} due to imperfect identification of victim rows.
        
        \subsubsection{Constraints} \label{subsubsec:limitations-outspacing-refresh}
            Several limitations affect mitigation strategies across both software and hardware approaches. Certain hammering 
            techniques can avoid cache misses, rendering detection ineffective. Detection heuristics frequently generate high 
            false-positive rates in real workloads. Mitigations may restrict system functionality or break compatibility with 
            existing software. Single-error correction remains insufficient, leaving multi-bitflips exploitable. Hardware 
            protections are often manufacturer-dependent (e.g., \textit{\gls{trr}}) and may degrade performance, increase energy consumption, 
            or be difficult to deploy on existing platforms, such as ARM architectures~\cite{paperTriggeringRHHardwareFault}. Software-based approaches 
            generally provide only partial workarounds and cannot fully overcome the physical limitations of \gls{dram}, often remaining 
            incomplete. Incorrect identification of victim rows can be exploited, as demonstrated by~\cite{paperTRRespass,paperHalfDouble}.
            \\
            % Conclusion
            To date, existing mitigation strategies only reduce the risk of Rowhammer attacks. No solution provides 
            comprehensive protection across all \gls{dram} generations and attack variants. Full protection would require 
            reliably tracking or constraining every row activation at the hardware level, independent of access patterns, 
            instruction choices, or \gls{dram} vendor behavior, to ensure that no combination of activations could bring 
            any victim cell close to its disturbance threshold.

    \subsection{TRR Bypass} \label{subsec:trr-bypass}
        Although the absence of observed bitflips points toward effective mitigation on Raspberry Pi platforms, 
        these devices were produced recently in 2020 and likely deploy an improved mitigation scheme, as noted in~\cite{paperBlacksmith}.
        Prior work research~\cite{paperHalfDouble, paperTRRespass, paperBlacksmith, paperTRRScope} 
        has demonstrated that protection mechanisms such as \gls{trr} can 
        often be circumvented through carefully engineered access patterns. To assess whether such 
        bypasses are relevant in this context, this section analyzes \gls{trr} behavior in detail and evaluates 
        the applicability of \gls{trr}-aware fuzzing techniques on Raspberry Pi devices.
        \\
        One of the most important terms for this is the \gls{mac}, which specifies the limit on the number of
        consecutive activations a single memory row can sustain within one refresh interval. When 
        the \gls{mac} is reached and further accesses exceed this boundary, the device either executes all 
        required refresh commands before allowing additional row activations or enters \gls{trr} mode to refresh only 
        the adjacent victim rows~\cite{paperTRRScope,datasheetLPDDR4}.
        \\
        \gls{trr} bypass can be attempted using Rowhammer fuzzers, which are designed to generate variations in 
        inputs or execution patterns that may provoke unexpected system behavior~\cite{webFuzzTests, webFuzzing}. Specifically, 
        in the context of Rowhammer, this approach targets the circumvention of TRRespass. For this purpose, 
        TRRespass is employed, what represents a scalable black-box Rowhammer fuzzer introduced in 2020 by 
        Frigo et al.~\cite{paperTRRespass}. It is designed for "\gls{trr}-aware" Rowhammer analysis and exploitation, primarily on 
        x86 systems. The tool automatically identifies access patterns capable of bypassing \gls{trr} based on two 
        parameters: cardinality and location.
        \\
        The use of hugepages is critical for TRRespass functionality. In modern processor architectures, the 
        \gls{cpu} and \gls{os} must keep track of mappings between virtual pages and their corresponding physical 
        memory locations. As the number of memory pages increases, locating the corresponding physical memory mappings become
        more time-consuming. Larger page sizes reduce the number of page entries that need to be 
        traversed~\cite{webHugepagesDebian}. TRRespass relies on hugepages to obtain large, physically contiguous memory regions, 
        which are necessary for reliably mapping virtual to physical addresses and targeting specific \gls{dram} 
        rows. Without hugepages, many hammering strategies either fail to function reliably or do not work at 
        all, as indicated in~\cite{paperTRRespass}.
        \\
        For the evaluation of TRRespass, three out of five test devices were successfully used. The remaining 
        two devices, referred to as Raspberry Pi 5 with 4 and 8~GB RAM, encountered an unexpected error during the pre-allocation of a single 1~GB hugepage, which 
        could not be resolved even after rebuilding the kernel multiple times. Due to the underlying smaller 
        hugepage sizes, TRRespass only works suboptimally and less reliably, which is why these results are 
        not discussed. On the devices where hugepages were functional, TRRespass was executed with varying 
        parameters, access patterns, and \gls{dram} mappings, including reverse-engineered mappings as well as 
        those provided by~\cite{paperDRAMA, paperMemoryAware, paperDRAMMaUT}. 
        Across all evaluated devices, we did not observe any effective access pattern capable of inducing bitflips.
        Even after multiple experiments varying in runtime length of 5 to 10 and in some cases also over 20 hours 
        all tested access patterns, including N-sided, 2-sided, and 3-sided, results in no observable or reproducible 
        bitflips on any of our test devices
        \\
        These findings suggest that the Raspberry Pi 4 and 5 models 
        are either not vulnerable to Rowhammer attacks or exhibit the same limitations observed in certain 
        LPDDR4x devices studied by~\cite{paperBlacksmith}. Additional details are discussed in 
        \cref{sec:discussion-and-limitations}.
